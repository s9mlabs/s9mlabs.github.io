01First principles

02Back to first principles

Where brains
become blueprints.

To build more intelligent, capable and safe systems, we start from the only intelligence that works.

03Dusk · our research program

We measure
living tissue.

We record from living neurons and nervous systems directly: voltage and current, what switches on and when, how strongly a cell responds, what it is allowed to learn, and what each of those costs in energy. Every principle we find is written down as a datasheet of measured ranges, the way an engineer documents a part.

Rules, not weights. Measured in living cells, not assumed from a textbook.

04The bio-intelligent architecture

Measured insight,
two prongs.

Everything we measure funnels into one thing: a bio-intelligent architecture, a way of organizing computation taken from living brains rather than from a textbook. From it we build in two directions at once. Foundation models that do more work per watt, from less data, and keep learning after they ship. And chips co-designed to run those models natively. The same measurements feed both, and each new principle makes both better.

Safety comes with the structure: what a system can do and learn is set by rules you can inspect, not by filters bolted on afterward.

06Where we start

Drones
first.

Robots and drones come first, because power is their tightest limit. A drone has a few watts to think with, so a machine that learns in the field, keeps what it learned and runs on a battery wins there long before it wins a benchmark. Then robots. Then the data center, where most of the world’s compute sits.

Something we didn’t cover? Write to team@s9mlabs.com.

Electrical activity in living nervous systems: voltage and current, what switches on and when, how strongly a cell responds, what it is allowed to learn, and what each of those costs in energy. Every principle is written down as a datasheet of measured ranges, the way an engineer documents a part.

The rules are measured, not learned. We test every measured rule against a copy of the system that learns the same setting for itself, and the thesis stands only when the measured version wins. The system also keeps learning after it ships, where it is, without a retraining step and without a data center in the loop.

Parts of it are being done, separately. Neuroscience labs measure living circuits but rarely build products from what they find. AI labs build on the 1943 neuron because it runs fast on GPUs and keeps improving with scale, so there is little pressure to start over. Neuromorphic chips borrow the brain’s spikes but not its measured rules. Building every layer from measurements takes all three fields working as one team, and that combination is rare.

Two things changed. Recording living circuits in detail has become routine and affordable, so the measurements are within reach of a small team. And the cost of running today’s AI, in power and in data, has become one of the main limits on where it can go. The first makes this work possible; the second makes it worth doing.

Because the principles do not map well onto hardware built for matrix multiplication. A chip designed together with the models, from the same measurements, does far less work per decision and spends almost nothing moving data around. That is where most of the energy goes today.

In a brain, paths that never switch on cannot be taken. We build from the same rules, so what a system can do and what it is allowed to learn are set by its structure, which can be inspected, rather than by filters added after training. Safety is part of the architecture, not a layer on top.

We do not sell a chatbot. We supply the architecture, the models and the chips that other companies build their products on: AI labs, and the makers of robots, drones and edge devices. Together they serve every person and business that uses AI.

The team covers the whole path the work needs: recording living cells, turning what they do into models, and designing the hardware that runs them. Our scientific advisor, Peter Voss, is one of the pioneers of AGI: he co-coined the term. And because we start from measurements rather than from an existing model, every layer we build can be checked against the living system it came from.

Yes. Advancing the knowledge available to researchers, engineers and the public is the purpose s9m Labs PBC is chartered for. Our research tools are on GitHub, and the datasheets and papers are published as they mature.

How it works

We record living neurons as they respond and adapt, and we work out the rule each one is following: what switches on, when, and what it is allowed to learn. That rule, measured, is what we build from, instead of a trained weight.

The measurements become a datasheet. The datasheet becomes a model, and the model becomes a chip. A system built this way learns from one example, where it is, and keeps what it already knew. The details are ours for now; the datasheets are published on a delay.

The chip.

Co-designed with the models

Today’s accelerators are built for one operation: multiplying very large matrices, very fast, with the weights shuttled in and out of memory. The principles we measure do not look like that, so the hardware should not either.

Our chip is designed together with the models, from the same measurements. It learns in place, as things happen, and spends almost nothing moving data around. That is the gap between those two numbers, and it is where we are working: drones and robots first, where every watt matters, then the data center.
